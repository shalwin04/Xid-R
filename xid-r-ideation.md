# Xid-R — Agentic GPU Compute Broker

_Working brief for the All Things Agentic Hackathon (submission deadline August 31, 2026)_

## 1. Problem Statement

Two trends are colliding inside every enterprise that has bought into AI at scale, and neither is being solved by the same team.

The first is that enterprise GPU capacity sits idle far more than anyone budgeted for. The most current and widely cited figure comes from Cast AI's 2026 State of Kubernetes Optimization Report, which measured telemetry across roughly 23,000 clusters and found average GPU utilization sitting around 5%, with teams effectively paying for twenty times the capacity they use. That number has been picked up widely (VentureBeat's "$401 billion problem" framing, TechRadar, Arc Compute, Parasail), though it is worth being precise that nearly every secondary citation traces back to that same single Cast AI dataset rather than independent corroboration — DevZero, for comparison, puts typical GPU-cluster utilization at a less severe 15–25%. Separately, Parasail's own measured telemetry across 14 H100s over 18 days found that GPUs left idle but still allocated continue drawing 26–66W depending on memory type, which is a real, additional cost layer on top of the opportunity cost of unused compute. None of this waste is purely wasteful by accident, either — Dell has argued a meaningful share of it is structural, driven by data-gravity and regulatory copy restrictions rather than pure scheduling failure, and TechRadar has noted enterprises may be rationally hoarding capacity against long procurement lead times. The honest read is: the waste is real and large, but not all of it is reclaimable just by scheduling smarter.

The second trend is that AI agents are becoming a first-class, structurally different consumer of that same compute. S&P Global's November 2025 report found 58% of organizations are actively building agent capability, and cites GPU shipment forecasts revised upward more than 500% versus original 2023 projections specifically because agents issue chained, concurrent, machine-paced inference calls rather than the periodic, predictable bursts of traditional training jobs. Agent workloads are bursty and ephemeral by nature — a coding agent might need a GPU for six hours, a research agent for three days, and both need it now, not after a multi-week capacity request goes through procurement. The result is a familiar failure mode playing out at a new layer: teams either over-provision dedicated capacity for their agents (which then sits idle between turns, model calls, and human review pauses, compounding the very waste problem described above) or under-provision and bottleneck the agents they were trying to accelerate in the first place.

The specific, narrow problem this creates is that no existing system safely and automatically lets idle capacity flow to agent workloads on demand while preserving what the agent was doing when its host hardware gets reclaimed, in a way an enterprise's compliance and infrastructure teams can actually audit and trust. Solving discovery (where is the idle capacity), matching (whose request should get it), preemption (someone needs it back), and continuity (the agent shouldn't lose its work) together, for agent-shaped workloads specifically, is the gap.

## 2. Existing Landscape and the Gap

Two YC-backed startups sit on either side of this problem without covering both. Chamber (YC W26) is an AIOps copilot — an agent that diagnoses training failures and reclaims idle GPUs during CPU-heavy job phases — but it's built for internal ops teams watching their own cluster, surfaced through Slack, not a marketplace or an agent-facing provisioning layer. machine0 (YC S26) is a CLI/API that provisions persistent, per-minute-billed VMs for AI agents with MCP integration and suspend/resume, but it runs on ordinary DigitalOcean capacity rather than harvested or idle capacity, and its own Hacker News launch thread drew real skepticism about differentiation from a raw cloud API.

The most important finding from this research is the GKE Agent Sandbox row. Google already ships a mechanism strikingly close to the checkpoint model this brief lands on — workload-triggered, cooperative snapshotting that frees node capacity and claims real, large cost reductions. That is strong independent validation that both the problem and this general mechanism are real and actively being invested in by the platform itself. It also means the checkpoint mechanism cannot be the product's moat, because Google will keep improving it faster than any startup can. What nobody has built, including Google, is the layer above the mechanism: a cross-cluster, cross-tenant, framework-agnostic broker that decides whose idle capacity gets harvested and who gets it next, negotiates the handoff with whatever agent framework is actually running, and produces the accounting and audit trail an enterprise needs to turn any of this on in production. That is the whitespace this brief targets.

## 3. Solution Overview

Xid-R is an agentic broker that matches idle or underutilized Google Cloud GPU capacity to AI agent workloads on demand, using cooperative, agent-state-level checkpointing so agents survive preemption transparently, with a full audit and cost-accounting trail for enterprise governance.

The deliberate design choice underneath that sentence is that Xid-R does not try to reinvent GPU virtualization or checkpointing — it orchestrates mechanisms that already exist and are actively improving: GKE's native GPU time-sharing, NVIDIA MPS, and multi-instance GPU (MIG) for co-locating workloads on a shared GPU; GCE Spot VM preemption notices, configurable up to 120 seconds, as an opportunistic capacity source; Cloud Run's GPU-backed worker pools as instant serverless burst capacity; and GKE Agent Sandbox Pod Snapshots or NVIDIA's own cuda-checkpoint/CRIUgpu as the underlying save primitive where applicable. Building on real, documented, currently-shipping infrastructure rather than kernel-level engineering is what makes this achievable in a hackathon timeframe and credible to a judge with systems background — a claim like "sub-2-second lossless VRAM freeze via custom eBPF and CRIU" does not survive scrutiny, but "we orchestrate GKE's native GPU sharing plus a real 120-second Spot preemption window plus agent-cooperative state serialization" does, because every piece of it is independently verifiable.

The reason cooperative, application-layer checkpointing is the right model — rather than a kernel-level memory dump — is specific to the target workload. Unlike a generic CUDA process, an AI agent already carries explicit, structured, serializable state: a task queue, a scratchpad or conversation memory, intermediate artifacts. That means preemption can be a negotiated handshake rather than a blind freeze: Xid-R signals that capacity is needed back, the agent serializes its own state to a pre-assigned storage location, and resume is simply reloading that state on whatever capacity opens up next. This sidesteps the genuinely unsolved problem in this space — nobody has publicly demonstrated multi-node, cross-hardware live migration of a large distributed training job — because that isn't the problem Xid-R needs to solve. Its unit of work is a single agent sandbox, not a distributed training run, so single-node, cooperative, application-layer checkpointing is sufficient.

Capacity is harvested through three lanes. Spot-capacity harvesting runs agent sandboxes on Spot VMs and reacts to the real preemption notice from the instance metadata server. Idle reserved-capacity harvesting turns on GKE's native GPU time-sharing or MPS to backfill idle cycles on an enterprise's own committed node pools, stepping up to MIG hardware partitioning when the requesting agent isn't trusted enough for soft isolation. Instant serverless burst uses Cloud Run GPU worker pools when no idle capacity exists anywhere, so a request is never simply denied.

## 4. Architecture

The system diagram (delivered alongside this brief as `xidr-architecture.png`, and persisted as a Cowork artifact for ongoing revision) lays out five layers.

Any agent framework — a Claude Code session, an ADK-native agent, a LangGraph pipeline, or an agent hosted on machine0-style infrastructure — sits at the top as a consumer. It talks to a Self-Service Surface, a Cloud Run service implementing an MCP server plus a thin CLI, exposing `xidr_request_gpu`, `xidr_checkpoint_ack`, `xidr_release`, `xidr_status`, and `xidr_explain` as tools. Beneath that sits the Policy and Reasoning Engine, the actual intelligence of the system, built as a small team of cooperating ADK agents rather than a single monolith: a Capacity Scout that continuously reasons over utilization patterns in the Capacity Fabric and pre-stages capacity ahead of predicted idle windows; a Negotiator that handles the reclaim conversation; a Compliance and Critic agent that independently reviews scheduling decisions against policy as a check and balance rather than a rubber stamp; and a Cost and Value Analyst that turns raw lease data into dollar figures and proactive recommendations. Two peer systems sit beneath the reasoning engine: the Capacity Fabric, a Firestore-backed inventory continuously updated by pollers against Cloud Monitoring and DCGM metrics, the GKE API, the Compute Engine API, and — for non-GCP or on-prem capacity — a lightweight Xid-R telemetry sensor; and Checkpoint Orchestration, a thin adapter layer that calls whichever underlying save mechanism fits the workload (GKE Pod Snapshot, cuda-checkpoint/CRIUgpu, or the MCP-cooperative handshake) rather than reimplementing any of them. Every lease transition, from every layer, writes into an Accounting and Audit Ledger — Firestore rolling up nightly into BigQuery — which feeds both a dashboard for infrastructure teams and the `xidr_explain` tool, so a human can ask why any specific decision was made and get back the actual reasoning that was logged at decision time.

Two different protocols are used deliberately for two different relationships. MCP governs the tool-calling relationship between a consuming agent and Xid-R itself — an agent asks for a resource and gets one back, a one-directional request-response shape. A2A (Agent2Agent), Google's open protocol for direct agent-to-agent communication, governs the negotiation relationship during reclaim: when the Negotiator needs capacity back, it doesn't fire a command at a passive listener, it sends a real message to the tenant agent's advertised Agent Card — "I need this GPU back in 45 seconds, can you checkpoint in time, or would you rather I migrate you live to a Cloud Run worker right now" — and the tenant agent can genuinely respond rather than simply comply, wrapped in A2A's Task lifecycle of submitted, working, completed, or failed states. This distinction is not cosmetic: it is what makes the reclaim flow a real instance of autonomous, agentic behavior rather than infrastructure automation with a chatbot in front of it, and it is a direct, demonstrable hit on the hackathon's own "real autonomous action over chatbot functionality" judging criterion.

Four flows describe how data actually moves. In the request flow, an agent calls `xidr_request_gpu`, the Self-Service Surface publishes a `ComputeRequested` event, the Policy Engine matches it against harvestable capacity (reasoning with Gemini when more than one candidate is viable), and a Lease record is created and granted. In the idle-discovery flow, the Capacity Fabric independently notices a reserved node pool has been underutilized long enough to flip to harvestable, and the Policy Engine proactively grants against it from a backlog of queued requests. In the reclaim flow, a preemption signal — a Spot notice, a GKE eviction, or utilization climbing back up on a co-located node — arrives with a grace-period budget attached, the Negotiator engages the tenant agent over A2A, a successful checkpoint moves the lease to a checkpointed state and immediately re-runs the request flow to resume the agent elsewhere, and a failed checkpoint is marked lost in the ledger rather than silently hidden. In the accounting flow, every transition above writes a billing event with duration and baseline on-demand price, rolling up into the dollar figures the product ultimately sells on.

Trust is enforced through tiers rather than left to the requester's discretion: an internal, vetted agent can be co-located via MPS soft isolation, while an external or less-trusted agent is forced onto MIG hardware partitioning or a fully dedicated instance, and any node hosting a production-tagged primary workload refuses MPS co-location outright regardless of tier, because MPS provides no real memory protection between processes and a crashing harvested job can take a production job down with it. Several other failure modes surfaced during design review are handled by construction rather than left as known gaps: lease grants against a freed capacity unit are atomic to prevent double-booking when multiple requests contend for the same opening; idle detection requires sustained low utilization before a unit is marked harvestable, and a cooldown follows any reclaim, to prevent flapping that would otherwise inflate the "compute-hours reclaimed" metric without any real agent progress; the Policy Engine tracks each workload's observed checkpoint duration and refuses to grant a lease on capacity whose grace period is shorter than that workload can reliably checkpoint within; a checkpoint acknowledgment from an agent is independently verified against actual process or pod termination rather than trusted blindly, with a hard timeout eviction as backstop; checkpoint storage in GCS is retained on a short window with delta or compressed state where possible, so storage and egress costs don't quietly erode the savings the product is sold on; and request matching uses an explicit fairness policy rather than simple recency, so one aggressive internal team cannot monopolize every idle cycle that opens up.

## 5. Deployment Model

Because Xid-R schedules and evicts production GPU workloads and briefly holds checkpointed agent state that can carry proprietary code or data, the deployment model treats trust as a first-class design constraint rather than an afterthought. The system splits cleanly into a data plane and a control plane. Everything that touches live scheduling decisions and checkpoint content — the full agent team in the Policy and Reasoning Engine, the Capacity Fabric, Checkpoint Orchestration, the MCP and A2A endpoints, and the GCS bucket holding checkpoint state — deploys into the customer's own GCP project, using the customer's own service accounts and their own Gemini API quota, distributed as a Terraform module or GCP Marketplace listing rather than run by Xid-R on the customer's behalf. Only a thin, low-stakes control plane is hosted centrally: licensing and metered billing through GCP Marketplace, and an optional Advisor agent fed exclusively by anonymized, aggregate telemetry — utilization percentages, dollars recovered, policy-effectiveness metrics, never workload content or checkpoint data — that can tell a customer their reclaim success rate sits in the bottom third of comparable fleets, or suggest a grace-period adjustment based on patterns observed across the install base. This split resolves the tension between an easy-to-adopt, centrally-improvable product and an enterprise's legitimate refusal to let a third party's control plane touch their production GPUs or their agents' state directly, and distributing through GCP Marketplace also means billing rides Google's existing metering infrastructure and procurement trust rather than requiring a customer to grant a startup direct IAM access before any evaluation can even begin.

## 6. Hackathon Alignment

The primary submission track is Fortified Enterprise Fleet — "scalable, compliant institutional agent networks" is close to a literal description of what Xid-R is. All three required elements of the stack are used natively rather than bolted on: Gemini powers the actual reasoning in the Policy Engine's multi-agent team, ADK is the framework the agent team and the tenant-agent SDK are built on, and Cloud Run, Firestore, and Pub/Sub anchor the required Google Cloud service. Against the stated judging weights, the reclaim flow's A2A negotiation between the Negotiator and a tenant agent is the clearest demonstration of real autonomous action for the innovation criterion; the layered architecture, the deliberate MCP/A2A protocol split, and the explicit handling of contention, trust, and fairness speak to architectural discipline; and a live demo showing a real Spot preemption notice triggering an actual checkpoint and resume, narrated by `xidr_explain`, addresses production readiness more credibly than a simulated one would.

## 7. MVP Scope for the Build Window

Given roughly twelve days to the deadline, the buildable slice is a single GKE cluster with one GPU node pool, one Spot VM pool, and one Cloud Run GPU worker pool as the harvest targets; the Self-Service Surface and Policy Engine (Scheduler plus Negotiator, with Scout and Compliance as stretch goals) running as ADK agents on Cloud Run; two toy tenant agents built with ADK to demonstrate the negotiation and resume loop; the cooperative checkpoint SDK stub; and a small dashboard showing live leases, checkpoint and resume events, and a running dollars-recovered counter. The demo script: one harvested agent gets backfilled onto an idle GKE node pool via MPS, is throttled and cooperatively checkpointed when a higher-priority job reclaims the node, and resumes on a Cloud Run GPU worker pool; a second harvested agent on a Spot VM hits a real, not simulated, 120-second preemption notice, checkpoints to GCS, and resumes on a fresh Spot VM; and a live `xidr_explain` call during the recording asks the orchestrator why a specific eviction happened and gets back the Gemini-generated rationale that was logged at decision time.

## 8. Business Model

Distribution runs through the GCP Marketplace as a private, in-VPC deployable, billed through Google's own metered billing rather than a custom billing engine. Pricing combines a base platform license with a gain-share component on the net dollar value of compute harvested, calculated directly from the Accounting and Audit Ledger against each capacity unit's baseline on-demand price — the same ledger that answers audit questions doubles as the billing source of truth, which keeps the two stories consistent rather than requiring separate justification.

## 9. Naming

Xid is the term NVIDIA's own driver stack uses for the hardware and driver fault codes it logs when something goes wrong on a GPU — a token anyone who has run a GPU fleet at scale will recognize immediately, and a deliberate signal that this product speaks the operator's language rather than marketing language. It is also, separately, Postgres's term for a transaction identifier, which happens to fit a product whose core mechanic is giving a running execution a checkpointable identity. The R stands for Runtime. Working tagline: every idle cycle, checkpointed.
